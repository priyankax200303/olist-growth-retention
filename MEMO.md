# Recommendation: test retention, and treat delivery as a service-quality fix

**Decision needed:** where to focus the next quarter of growth effort.

**Recommendation:** run a controlled second-purchase test. Fix delivery failures separately, because of their link to bad reviews. Do not count on delivery work alone to raise repeat purchases.

## What the data shows
- **Growth rests on acquisition.** Returning customers provide 2.17% of GMV, rising to 2.74% in March to August 2018. Daily GMV fell 5.3%, from R$32.3k in March to May to R$30.5k from June to 22 August.
- **Customers rarely return.** The 180-day repeat rate is 1.99%. On-time, 5-star orders reach only 1.92%.
- **Late orders score far worse.** They average 2.27 stars vs 4.29, and are 6.7% of reviewed orders but 32.6% of 1-2 star reviews.
- **Delivery's link to repeat is unproven.** The adjusted gap is -0.33 points (95% interval -0.80 to +0.13). A meaningful effect on late customers cannot be ruled out, but few orders are late, so the total is limited.
- **Value does not predict return.** Among customers with full follow-up, the top 40% by first order value repeat at 1.95%, vs 2.03% for the rest.

## Sizing
Volumes use the 12 months to August 2018: 72,760 new customers, 7.8% of first orders late, and an average second order of R$127. All figures are within the sample. Lifts apply to the customers affected.

| Scenario | Lift | Added orders a year | Added GMV | Share of annual GMV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No late orders, central estimate | 0.33 pts | 19 | R$2.4k | 0.02% |
| No late orders, low end of interval | 1.02 pts | 58 | R$7.4k | 0.07% |
| Retention programme, 40% reach | 0.25 pts | 73 | R$9.2k | 0.09% |
| Retention programme, 40% reach | 0.5 pts | 146 | R$18.4k | 0.18% |
| Retention programme, 40% reach | 1 pt | 291 | R$36.9k | 0.36% |

Reach and retention lifts are assumptions, not forecasts. At 40% reach, a 0.2-point lift would match the delivery figure at the low end of its interval. No scenario here is large, so programme cost matters. Delivery's main value is likely in reviews and service quality, which this table does not capture.

## Ranked actions
1. **Confirm the baseline.** Recalculate repeat rates on complete order data, because this data is a sample. This decides how big the prize is.
2. **Run a randomised second-purchase test.** Start with recent, one-order customers: 36,720 people, 38.9% of customers. Test high-value customers as a separate arm rather than assuming they respond better, because value does not predict repeat here. Keep a holdout group and measure incremental orders and margin at 180 days. Test contact timing too: with a full year of follow-up, the median gap to a second order is 125 days, and 64% of those orders come within 180. Promote across sellers, since 75% of second orders go to a different seller. First confirm Olist can contact these customers, since sales run through partner marketplaces.
3. **Find out what went wrong in February and March 2018.** Those months hold 34.5% of late orders, with late rates of 14% and 19%, and the pattern did not appear a year earlier. Late orders spend about 20 days longer in transit, so start with carriers.
4. **Manage underperforming sellers.** Among sellers with 30 or more deliveries, late rates run from 1.5% at the 10th percentile to 12.8% at the 90th. Compare them on routes served before acting.
5. **Review promise dates on the worst routes.** AL, MA and SE have the highest late rates among states with meaningful volume. RJ has more late orders in total (1,495 vs 261 for those three combined), so it should be reviewed too.

## What would change this view
- Full data shows a much higher repeat rate.
- A properly powered study finds a larger delivery effect.
- The test shows no incremental lift, in which case focus should stay on acquisition and order value.
